## PATTERN NOTE

Sensor and captured data share a structural feature: the world writes itself into a record, but access is almost never automatic. The data's existence is determined by who owns the instrument — a satellite operator, a utility, a hospital, a police department — and that ownership almost always precedes any public interest consideration. The dominant power dynamic is asymmetric: organizations with physical infrastructure accumulate data continuously while subjects and publics learn of it only when access is contested. Environmental and geophysical data tends toward openness (USGS, NOAA, NASA have strong open-data traditions), while data touching individual bodies, private infrastructure, or law enforcement is routinely buried behind operational and liability arguments.

LLMs are changing this in two ways. First, they lower the threshold for interpreting data that already exists but requires specialist knowledge to read — hyperspectral imagery, seismic waveforms, pathology slides — turning buried data into actionable information without additional collection. Second, they enable synthesis across many sparse public sensors (weather stations, river gauges, satellite passes) that individually capture too little to be revealing, but together can reconstruct events no single instrument recorded. The next frontier of sensor data access is less about new instruments and more about who gets to run inference on the archives that already exist.
