# Initial Draft Summary

## Context

A conversation with Claude exploring the concept of **information asymmetry** — who has valuable information, why others can't access it, and what could change that.

---

## 1. Valuable Information People Pay For (70 examples)

A broad taxonomy of information that commands a price, spanning:
- **Financial**: institutional order flow, dark pool trades, pre-IPO financials, real-time bond data
- **Health**: genetic risk, doctor malpractice records, drug trial results, outbreak maps
- **Real estate**: off-market listings, zoning changes, foreclosure filings
- **Legal/Government**: sealed records, regulatory pre-announcements, lobbying transcripts
- **Business intelligence**: competitor pricing, supply chain sourcing, customer churn
- **Personal data**: data broker profiles, location history, shadow profiles
- **Science**: unpublished trial data, paywalled research, satellite imagery
- **Geopolitical**: diplomatic cables, sanctions networks, polling internals

Key insight: **the most valuable information is typically held by actors who benefit from others not having it** — insurers, employers, platforms, governments.

---

## 2. Why Information Has Value — 7 Use Cases

Information ultimately does one of three things: reduces uncertainty, creates an edge, or enables control. The seven concrete uses are:

1. **Financial gain** — trading on better/earlier information (legal to illegal spectrum)
2. **Risk reduction** — avoiding bad decisions (health, career, purchases)
3. **Competitive advantage** — knowing what competitors are doing
4. **Personal life optimization** — better decisions on career, health, relationships, education
5. **Legal/political power** — litigation, lobbying, regulatory navigation
6. **Privacy protection** — knowing what others know about you
7. **Epistemic edge** — more accurate mental models that compound over time

The deepest insight: **better information → better models → better future decisions → more resources to acquire more information** — a compounding flywheel of elite advantage.

---

## 3. Barriers to Information Flow

Information barriers are rarely accidental — they are **architectures of advantage** maintained by those who benefit from the current knowledge distribution. Seven categories:

1. **Legal/regulatory** — IP law, securities law, privacy law, classification, professional privilege
2. **Economic** — paywalls, deliberate scarcity, competitive moats, cost of collection
3. **Institutional** — compartmentalization, liability avoidance, internal politics, regulatory capture
4. **Cognitive** — overload, confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, various forms of illiteracy
5. **Technical** — format lock-in, digital divide, algorithmic gatekeeping
6. **Political/power** — state censorship, manufactured uncertainty, elite networks, chilling effects
7. **Temporal** — reporting lags, publication delays, embargo systems

To understand any barrier: *"If this information flowed freely, who would lose power, money, or control?"*

---

## 4. Where LLMs Change the Game (60 examples)

LLMs are **information asymmetry destroyers** — they compress expertise, bypass gatekeepers, and make previously unusable knowledge actionable. The shift is from *information existing* to *information being usable*.

Key domains affected:
- **Legal**: contract analysis, rights navigation, court filing, regulatory compliance
- **Medical**: diagnosis context, medical record interpretation, rare disease recognition, insurance appeals
- **Financial**: tax optimization, debt restructuring, benefits optimization, credit disputes
- **Education**: university-level tutoring, research synthesis, language barriers, test prep
- **Civic**: legislation analysis, lobbying disclosure, FOIA drafting, local planning documents
- **Business**: regulatory navigation, grant writing, employment law, supply chain risk

Three tiers of impact:
- **Tier 1 — Democratization**: information existed but only experts could use it
- **Tier 2 — Synthesis**: information existed in fragments; LLMs connect it
- **Tier 3 — Power redistribution**: LLMs give individuals tools only institutions had

---

## 5. Does an Atlas of Information Exist?

**Partial attempts exist, but nothing comprehensive.**

Closest existing tools:
- Wikipedia/Wikidata — encyclopedic, not structural
- Domain silos: PubMed, Westlaw, Bloomberg, OpenCorporates — fragmented, often paywalled
- GDELT — real-time news events globally (underutilized)
- OpenAlex — 250M+ scholarly works, academic only

Why a true atlas has never been built:
- Those who could build it benefit from opacity
- Information is not static — requires constant updating
- Classification across domains is unsolved
- Power structures would resist being mapped
- The most valuable information leaves no public trace

**A complete atlas would need 7 layers:**

| Layer | What | Status |
|-------|------|--------|
| 1 | What information exists | Partial (Google, Wikipedia) |
| 2 | Where it lives | Partial (data.gov, OpenAlex) |
| 3 | Who can access it and at what cost | Almost nothing |
| 4 | Who controls/suppresses it | Essentially nothing |
| 5 | How reliable is it | Nascent (Retraction Watch) |
| 6 | How does it flow between actors | Almost nothing |
| 7 | What should exist but doesn't | Nobody has attempted this |

LLMs approximate layers 1–2 but fail on 3–7: no source mapping, no power mapping, no gap mapping, static, and prone to hallucination.

---

## Core Thesis

The world is missing foundational infrastructure: **a living, open, multi-layered atlas of information** mapping what exists, where it lives, who controls it, what it costs, how reliable it is, what's missing, and how it flows.

This would be a **map of power itself** — which is why it doesn't exist, and why building it would be among the most important and most resisted projects of our time. The analogy: what OpenStreetMap did for physical geography needs to happen for the information landscape.
