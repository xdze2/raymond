## What It Is

ShotSpotter — rebranded as SoundThinking in 2023 — operates a network of acoustic sensors (microphones) mounted on utility poles, rooftops, and streetlights across more than 150 cities in the United States, plus deployments in South Africa and the Bahamas. When the system detects a loud impulsive sound, it timestamps the audio, calculates a position using time-difference-of-arrival across multiple sensors, and runs it through a classification algorithm to determine whether the event was a gunshot, a firework, or other acoustic event. The output — a "ShotSpotter alert" — is a structured incident record: GPS coordinates, timestamp, a confidence score, a classification label, and optionally a short audio clip. These alerts are pushed to police dispatch in real time, typically within 30–60 seconds of the sound.

What makes this data unusual is its stratification. The raw audio recorded by sensors is retained by SoundThinking on its servers. The derived alert record — coordinates, time, classification — is shared with the subscribing police department. A third layer exists: human reviewers at SoundThinking's review center can manually reclassify events, and these reclassifications are logged internally. The gap between what the sensors captured and what the police department receives is substantial, and that gap is entirely controlled by SoundThinking.

## Who Controls It

SoundThinking holds the authoritative data. Raw audio is not transmitted to police departments — it stays on SoundThinking's infrastructure, accessible to the company's analysts and producible only when SoundThinking chooses to share it (e.g., in response to a subpoena or at a police department's request for a specific incident). Alert records are held by police departments as operational data, but they're often exempt from public records disclosure under law enforcement exemptions. SoundThinking's contracts with cities contain non-disclosure provisions that restrict departments from discussing the system's methodology publicly.

This creates an unusual accountability void: the evidence produced by ShotSpotter — audio clips and alert reports — has been used in criminal prosecutions, but the underlying algorithm, training data, and reviewer practices are proprietary trade secrets that defendants cannot inspect. Cities pay SoundThinking roughly $65,000–$95,000 per square mile per year for coverage; the vendor's revenue depends on continued police contracts, which creates structural incentives against transparency about false positive rates.

## What It Reveals

ShotSpotter data reveals two things simultaneously: the acoustic event that triggered the sensor, and the geography of police response. The spatial distribution of sensor deployments — concentrated in low-income and majority-Black neighborhoods — means the system doesn't produce a neutral map of gunfire; it produces a map of where the city has chosen to listen. Areas outside coverage zones generate no alerts regardless of activity.

Forensic analysis of ShotSpotter alerts in specific cities has revealed that the majority of alerts do not result in the discovery of gunshot evidence — studies in Chicago (Mac Arthur Justice Center, 2021) found that 89% of ShotSpotter alerts led to no gun, no victim, and no evidence of a shooting. This points to a significant false positive rate that is not publicly audited. For courts, ShotSpotter evidence has been used to establish probable cause, reconstruct timelines, and contradict defendant testimony — all based on an algorithm whose error rate is trade-secret-protected. The data reveals, structurally, that a private company is producing forensic evidence for criminal prosecutions under conditions that would not survive independent scientific review.

## Current Access Landscape

**Who has it:** SoundThinking (all raw audio and derived data), subscribing police departments (alert records for their jurisdiction), prosecutors (via discovery in specific cases), SoundThinking's human review team (real-time access to reclassifications).

**Who doesn't:** Defense attorneys (absent specific discovery requests, which SoundThinking often contests), journalists, researchers, city oversight bodies, and the public have no systematic access. Even city council members often cannot access performance data because it falls under law enforcement exemptions.

**Partial access points:** In specific criminal cases, courts have sometimes compelled SoundThinking to produce audio clips and classification logs through subpoenas; results are case-specific and not aggregated publicly. The MacArthur Justice Center (Chicago), the Surveillance Technology Oversight Project (STOP), and the ACLU have obtained partial information through litigation and public records requests. Some cities have commissioned independent audits — San Francisco's 2022 audit preceded its contract termination.

**Historical leaks or ruptures:** In 2021, Vice Motherboard published an investigation revealing that ShotSpotter reviewers had manually altered alert classifications and locations after the fact, in at least one case changing an alert's location in a way that contradicted a defendant's alibi. This was the most significant breach of SoundThinking's internal process to become public. In 2022, the city of Chicago obtained internal ShotSpotter documents through litigation that revealed the company's review practices more explicitly than any prior disclosure.

## Cracks & Pressure Points

- **Regulatory pressure:** No federal agency regulates acoustic surveillance networks. State-level surveillance technology transparency laws (Illinois SAUCL, California SB 34 analogs) require city council approval for new surveillance technologies but don't mandate data disclosure or independent algorithm audits.
- **Investigative journalism:** Vice Motherboard's 2021 investigation into manual reclassification was the seminal rupture; The Intercept has covered ShotSpotter's contract terms and evidentiary use; Chicago Tribune reporting on the system's performance preceded Chicago's 2024 contract non-renewal.
- **Litigation:** *People v. Johnson* (Illinois, 2022) was a landmark case in which ShotSpotter evidence was challenged on Daubert grounds; the motion to exclude was denied but drew significant attention to the algorithm's opacity. MacArthur Justice Center v. City of Chicago (ongoing) seeks broader disclosure of performance data.
- **Technical circumvention:** No technical circumvention is meaningful for subjects of surveillance. For researchers, gunshot detection can be approximated using open-source acoustic classification models and publicly available audio datasets, but without the proprietary training data and sensor network, replication is impossible.
- **Market alternatives:** Fusus and Genetec offer competing gunshot detection products; EAGL Technology markets a sensor network with different contractual terms. None offers substantially more public transparency. The competitive market provides no pressure toward openness.

## LLM & AI Impact

ShotSpotter's classification problem — distinguishing gunshots from fireworks, backfires, and construction — is exactly the kind of task where ML models have been state-of-the-art for a decade. SoundThinking already uses machine learning extensively; the addition of LLMs changes little at the classification layer. Where LLMs could matter is in the audit and accountability layer: given access to alert logs, reclassification records, and ground-truth outcome data (did police find evidence?), an LLM could identify systematic patterns in when human reviewers override algorithmic classifications — the exact analysis that journalists and litigants have been trying to perform manually for years. The barrier to that analysis is access to SoundThinking's internal data, not the sophistication of the analysis tool. LLMs also don't help with the audio itself: forensic acoustic analysis of gunshot recordings requires specialized models trained on acoustic physics, not language understanding. Impact: **low** — the bottleneck is access and legal disclosure, not analytical capability.
